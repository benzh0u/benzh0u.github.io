More thoughts on college VS. startups.

August 2025

In tech circles, the advice is seductive: skip college and learn by building. Peter Thiel founded an entire fellowship around this idea, paying students $100,000 to drop out and start companies instead. The success stories are compelling—founders of Cal.com, Scale AI, and Cluely all took this path and built centi-million/billion-dollar businesses before their peers graduated.

After spending a year and a half building my own startup, I understand the appeal. Why sit through biology lectures when I could be shipping products, learning to code by actually coding, and potentially making real money? The learning curve is steeper when the stakes are real. Every mistake costs you users, every breakthrough compounds into growth. That kind of pressure teaches you faster than any textbook.

But there's another perspective worth considering.

Paul Graham, who's advised more successful startups than perhaps anyone through Y Combinator, argues that college's real value isn't job training—it's intellectual exploration. College gives you permission to follow curiosity and wonder, something that becomes much harder once a startup consumes your life.

The examples are striking. Steve Jobs' calligraphy class led to the Mac's revolutionary typography, making it the first computer with beautiful fonts. Brian Chesky's design background became central to Airbnb's success; without that design sensibility, would strangers really trust each other enough to share homes? Robin Li, Baidu's founder, studied library science, exactly the kind of information organization skills needed to build China's dominant search engine.

In each case, seemingly irrelevant college experiences became competitive advantages. These founders didn't just learn random skills; they developed unique ways of seeing and solving problems that others missed.

Paul also made the case that if you're capable of building a successful company at 18, you'll likely build an even better one at 22, with more knowledge, better judgment, and a clearer sense of what you actually want to work on.

That's something to look out for, committing years to solving a problem I don't deeply care about. My last startup taught me enormous amounts about sales, how startups work in general, and about myself. But it also showed me how important it is to work on something that genuinely excites me for the long haul.

For now, I'm staying in college, but with a different mindset. I'm treating it as an R&D lab: exploring ideas, meeting potential co-founders, and building things on the side. If the right opportunity emerges, something I'd want to spend the next decade building, I'll drop everything to pursue it.

College isn't just about learning; it's about optionality. And optionality, when I'm young, might be more valuable than optimization.

Though I might feel differently about this trade-off if I were paying US tuition rates—where four years of college costs more than a small pre-seed rounds.
