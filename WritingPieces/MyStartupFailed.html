My Startup Failed. Here's what I've learned.

June 2025

Introduction

Last month, the startup I’ve been building for a year and half failed. I’m writing this blog to remind myself of some of the most important lessons.

Timrep (prev. Compliancy) was built by two high school best friends while wanting to start a startup. We both wanted to get our hands dirty instead of spending 4 years going through college.

We worked on tackling a real problem, got traction quickly, and raised funds from some of the best investors. It was a real learning experience that changed me in many ways, but we decided to shut down after a year and half due to co-founder separation.

So here are the three key takeaways of the last year and half.


Get started. Learn by doing.

A problem I’ve always had starting out was always reading and learning about business but never starting. The problem with that is that if you keep consuming without getting your hands dirty, you’ll feel like there’s too much to learn, and you’ll never start.

Plus, much of the learning is literally going through the suffering yourself. If you don’t, the lessons won’t relate to you and you won’t be able to use them. So, if there's something you want to get better at, just do it.


Choose a good co-founder, not just a good friend.

Building a startup is hard, and if the team isn’t right, it won’t work.

At first, our story seemed perfect. We were high school best friends. He could build anything to make the customers happy while I brought them in. We knew each other well and will do whatever it takes to make it work.

But friendship doesn’t equal fit under pressure. We’d never worked together in a high-stress environment, and few things are more intense than an early-stage startup where every other day feels like a fire drill.

In the end, we split as co-founders but stayed close friends. He’s one of the smartest and hardest-working people I know, and I have nothing but respect for him. Next time, I’ll make sure I’ve worked with my co-founder under pressure before jumping in.


Talk to your customers.

Your customers are all that matters. If you solve a real problem and make customers happy, raising money is easy. Without happy customers, money just buys time to reach product-market fit. In the early days, stay close to your users, work from their office if you can. Keep feedback loops tight. Plenty of startups raised from the best investors and died because they couldn’t delight their users.


Be lean.

We started with $40K, lived in a cheap apartment, and cooked bulk Costco meals. It wasn’t comfortable, but it doubled our runway from 6 to 12 months.

That said, if you’re cutting costs, don’t cut safety. We lived across from a victim services center in Little Haiti—our car was broken into and packages were stolen. Be lean, but use common sense. Don’t go to extremes like we did.


Don't focus on funding.

Some say investors are just money. Others say they’re game changers. It’s true that funding matters most—but good investors definitely help. I was lucky. Mine were supportive even when things went wrong for many months. If you can, raise from people you’d actually want to work with. Good investors matter.


Take care of your health.

This one sounds obvious, but I used to skip sleep to hit deadlines. I still remember near the end of October, there were two weeks where my co-founder and I averaged 4 hours a night. Sometimes we’d both wake up with chest pains. Looking back, we would’ve worked better and probably gotten a lot more done if we’d just rested. You can’t think clearly without sleep. Rest matters.


Look at accelerators.

Whether you’re new to startups or not, a good accelerator helps you avoid mistakes. Eliam, Angie, and Kris at Telora pushed me when I needed it and supported me when things got tough. They taught me a lot, even beyond startups. I think very highly of them. Reach out if you want to hear more.


What I plan to do going forward.

I want to keep building. I enjoy the challenge. I’m comfortable with risk and generally believe that I’ll be okay no matter where I end up.

For the next company, I’ll still lead on sales. I’ve done door-knocking for a year. Sales is what I’m best at. As Cyrus from useHaven said, startups come down to two things: being great at sales or great at building. Given my experience, I just want to become technical too, so I can understand the product and can run on my own in case anything happens.

For now, I’m going back to university to mostly to meet other builders, and eventually launch something new, solo or with a strong technical co-founder. Ideally, I’ll bootstrap until it’s profitable.


Godspeed,
Ben
